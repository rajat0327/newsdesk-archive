Training & Efficiency

TRACE: Rollout-Guided Quantization-Aware Training for FP4 Reinforcement Learning of MoE Language Models

This paper addresses the substantial computation and memory overheads in reinforcement learning (RL) for post-training large language models (LLMs), particularly during rollout generation. It identifies a key limitation in existing FP4 RL methods, which optimize quantization accuracy on training and rollout paths independently. The proposed TRACE method aims to directly reduce these overheads through rollout-guided quantization-aware training.

Why it matters — Directly optimizing quantization accuracy for both training and rollout paths in FP4 RL can significantly reduce computational and memory overheads for post-training LLMs, improving efficiency.

Training & Efficiency

Self-Generated Feedback Destabilizes Test-Time Training: A Causal Decomposition of Long-Horizon Adaptation

This paper investigates the destabilizing effect of self-generated feedback in test-time training (TTT), where a model learns from its own outputs during inference. It demonstrates that retaining generated-text updates worsens prediction on independent human-written text across long-horizon adaptation streams. The study provides a causal decomposition to explain how each update changes the model that generates subsequent training examples.

Why it matters — Learning from self-generated feedback in test-time training can destabilize models and degrade performance on independent data, highlighting a critical challenge for long-horizon adaptation strategies.

Agents & Tools

From Evidence to Action: How Tool-Using Agents Fail

This paper studies failure modes in tool-using agents, specifically examining breakdowns in the "evidence-to-action chain." It highlights that correct outcomes do not always guarantee that an agent's actions were supported by evidence established beforehand. The research analyzes these breaks across ten model-harness configurations, from single actions to dependent workflows.

Why it matters — Tool-using agents can achieve correct outcomes without their actions being supported by prior evidence, revealing a critical disconnect in the evidence-to-action chain that requires careful diagnosis beyond outcome metrics.

Evaluation & Benchmarks

LMBuild: Evaluating LLM Agents for Generating Buildable and Functional Structures

This paper introduces LMBuild, a new benchmark for evaluating LLM agents that generate 3D structures. It addresses the gap in existing evaluations, which primarily focus on geometric quality while overlooking the crucial aspects of physical realizability and intended function. LMBuild aims to assess whether generated objects can actually be built and perform their functions in the physical world.

Why it matters — Evaluating LLM agents for 3D structure generation requires assessing buildability and functionality, not just geometric quality, to ensure practical applicability and reveal real-world limitations.

Multimodal

PerturBot: Breaking Shortcut Priors in Vision-Language-Action Models with Perturbative Training

This paper addresses the problem of vision-language-action (VLA) policies completing complex tasks by relying on shortcut priors instead of relevant evidence. It provides examples where VLA models ignore instructed targets or execute actions based on familiar nouns despite changed verbs. The work proposes "perturbative training" as a method to break these shortcut priors and enhance model robustness.

Why it matters — Vision-language-action models can exploit shortcut priors, leading to actions unsupported by evidence; perturbative training is a technique to break these priors and enhance model robustness against such failures.

LLMs & Reasoning

Optimizing the Optimizer: Language Models Discover Faster Molecular Relaxation

This paper investigates the potential of language models to improve geometry optimization, a major and costly step in quantum-chemical workflows. It explores whether an LLM agent can discover faster molecular relaxation methods through "autoresearch" by rewriting existing optimizers. The goal is to reduce the wall time dominated by force evaluations at the density-functional level.

Why it matters — Language models can potentially discover and rewrite optimizers to accelerate computationally expensive scientific processes like molecular relaxation in quantum chemistry, demonstrating a novel application of "autoresearch."
